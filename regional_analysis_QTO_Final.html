3.2 GAM analysis

Data preparation (normalization)

  1. This code block normalizes fossil counts across sites and constructs the final dataset for comparative modeling. A custom function (normalize_counts) is defined to scale raw counts relative to the maximum within each panel, producing standardized values (counts_norm). To address boundary cases where normalized values equal 0 or 1, a small‑sample variance correction is applied (counts_norm_sv), ensuring statistical stability. The dataset is then partitioned by site (Punta Maroma, Florida, Belize, Saint Croix) and aggregated by centennial age bins, with associated climatic and disturbance predictors summarized as means. Each subset is normalized and labeled with its respective panel name. A regional dataset is additionally created by pooling all sites, normalized in the same manner, and explicitly tagged as “Regional.” Finally, all site‑level and regional subsets are merged into a consolidated dataset (merged_df_g), which provides harmonized counts and predictor variables for subsequent generalized additive modeling and comparative analyses.

Because of our interest in assessing temporal trends in continuous variables (e.g., temperature, hurricane frequency), we employ a Beta regression family with a logit link function and regression splines. This family regression distribution also responds to the inherent properties of the selected response variable which is bounded between 0 and 1. Finally, we define reference baselines for the thermal and hydro climatic predictors and set the model baseline to conditions ‘warmer’ than the 18th-century average, ‘wet’ hydro climatic periods, and ‘calm’ hurricane intervals. By establishing these baselines, we enable the model to accurately capture the effects of known periods of heightened climate variability, such as the Little Ice Age or the Caribbean Warm Period in concordance with our goals.

Model performance analysis encompassed a step-wise approach. Prior to model fitting, each site‑specific dataset was screened for detecting and suppressing statistical outliers in coral density and mean hurricane intensity. To that, we use Tukey’s interquartile range criterion. Filtering and removing outliers helps minimizing undue influence of extreme values and improve model stability. After outliers’ filtering, we assess the marginal effect of each potential explanatory variable by constructing seven univariate GAMs to evaluate independent, isolated effects. Subsequently, we built a suite of multivariate GAMs to assess the conditional, combined effects of variables in the presence of increasing numbers of variables and variable interactions.

To identify the optimal configuration in both cases, we applied a competitive Akaike Information Criterion threshold (∆AIC <2) alongside the principle of model parsimony. Upon selecting the overall optimal configuration, we conduct a rigorous quality assessment, including residual analysis, accuracy testing, and cross-validation for model stability. 

3.2.1 Evaluating marginal effects. Results.

To test the degree of concordance between local A. palmata population trajectories and regional climate trends, we first investigate the explanatory power of climatic predictor marginal (isolated) effects of both types, smooth and linear. When evaluating these marginal effects, models indicate temperature anomalies and centennial hurricane average impacts metrics along with thermal and Hydrodynamics trends where helpful indicators of coral density responses. As such, explained deviances yield values between 0.4 (regional) -90% (Punta Maroma, Mx)(Suppl. Material 1, Supp. Table 1, Supp.Fig.1). Nevertheless, biological organisms are influenced by the synergistic interplay of multiple climatic factors rather than isolated variables, so we next explore the combined effects following same approach.  

  1. Next three code blocks implement generalized additive models (GAMs) to evaluate the marginal effects of climatic and disturbance predictors on normalized coral fossil densities. A set of candidate formulas (formulas_mg) is defined, each incorporating age and a single predictor (temperature anomalies, rates of change, hurricane frequency and averages, or categorical climatic states). A helper function (fit_gams_panel) fits GAMs for each formula within a given panel using a beta regression with logit link, while compare_gams extracts Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values, computes ΔAIC relative to the best model, and derives model weights. The procedure is applied across all panels (local sites and regional aggregate), and results are consolidated into stats_all. Tabular summaries are generated with the gt package, and diagnostic plots display ΔAIC values and model weights, with a dashed threshold line (ΔAIC = 2) indicating competitive models. A refined function (fit_best_gam) is then introduced to fit, summarize, and extract key statistics from the best‑performing models at each site, including explained deviance, adjusted R², significant terms, and effective degrees of freedom. The outputs are compiled into a comparison table and exported as a publication‑ready PDF, while supplementary figures visualize relative model performance. This chunk thus provides a systematic framework for model selection and interpretation, identifying the most influential climatic and disturbance predictors of coral fossil density across spatial scales.

4.2 Model validation

4.2.1 5-Fold Cross validation Results (Marginal effects)

The cross-validation of marginal models revealed that marginal models capture local correlations but lack stable predictive capacity across folds. Model stability is highly dependent on data quality and resolution, with regional aggregation offering the only panel that sustains a weak, but positive explanatory signal.

The five‑fold cross‑validation revealed highly variable and often unstable signals across sites. In Belize, performance fluctuated dramatically: while one fold achieved very high fit (R² ≈ 0.99, Deviance Explained ≈ 0.88), the average across folds was modest (mean R² = 0.29, mean Deviance Explained = −0.13), reflecting inconsistency and limited generalization. Florida was consistently weak, with low R² (mean = 0.13) and strongly negative Deviance Explained (mean = −1.93), indicating that models failed to outperform a simple mean‑based prediction.

At Punta Maroma, with its high-resolution sampling database, results were particularly erratic: one fold showed strong fit (R² ≈ 0.79, Deviance Explained ≈ 0.71), yet others collapsed with extreme negative Deviance Explained (down to −22.8), yielding an unstable average (mean R² = 0.29, mean Deviance Explained = −7.15). In contrast, the Regional panel maintained the most consistent though modest performance, with positive Deviance Explained in several folds and an overall mean R² = 0.30, mean Deviance Explained = 0.11.

The cross‑validation of combined GAMs revealed a more nuanced but still unstable pattern across sites and confirm that while combined models can capture complex climatic signals, their stability is highly site‑dependent. 4.5 Residuals’ Temporal Independency Analysis

The residual independency analysis underscores clear site‑specific differences in temporal autocorrelation. While Punta Maroma and Regional models achieve residual independence, Belize demonstrates acceptable independence, whereas Florida retains moderate autocorrelation. These diagnostics confirm that temporal dependence is site‑specific, with Florida emerging as the most problematic case.

At Punta Maroma (M4_av), residuals show slight negative autocorrelation (φ_AR1 = −0.31, DW = 2.50, p = 0.91; Ljung–Box p = 0.63), indicating overall independence and confirming that the model captures local dynamics without leaving temporal dependence. The Regional model (M7_av) also achieves residual independence, with slightly negative autocorrelation (φ_AR1 = −0.22, DW = 2.39, p = 0.81; Ljung–Box p = 0.46), suggesting that aggregated signals, despite diluting site‑specific variability, retain a stable temporal structure. Hence, these two models can be interpreted with confidence.

For Belize (M6_fqcy), residuals are close to independence (φ_AR1 = 0.06, DW = 1.83, p = 0.31; Ljung–Box p = 0.70), showing no significant evidence of autocorrelation and suggesting acceptable stability. This site‑specific model provides useful, though somewhat less robust, inference.

In contrast, Florida (M7_av) exhibits moderate positive autocorrelation (φ_AR1 = 0.27, DW = 1.44, p = 0.027; Ljung–Box p = 0.11), consistent with short‑term dependence and weaker temporal fit. This residual pattern requires caution: additional model refinement (e.g., incorporating correlation structures or alternative predictors) may be necessary to avoid biased inference.

4.6 Concurvity analysis

Concurvity diagnostics reveal marked differences in predictor redundancy across sites. At Punta Maroma (M4_av), concurvity for s(age) is essentially negligible (observed ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻³³), indicating near‑complete independence of age as a predictor. This underscores the robustness of the local specification, as age contributes unique explanatory power without overlap.

In Florida (M7_av), concurvity is high for s(age) (observed ≈ 0.92) but low for hurricane impact average (s(Hur100_av) ≈ 0.27). This suggests that while age shares explanatory variance with other predictors, hurricane effects provide independent information, reinforcing their interpretive value.

For Belize (M6_fqcy), concurvity values are moderate (observed ≈ 0.71–0.83), reflecting partial redundancy between age and hurricane predictors. This indicates that while the model captures relevant signals, predictor overlap may limit interpretability and requires cautious inference.

At the regional scale (M7_av), concurvity for s(age) is high (observed ≈ 0.92), consistent with strong redundancy in aggregated datasets. However, hurricane predictors retain moderate independence (estimate ≈ 0.67–0.82), supporting their inclusion in combined models to capture non‑redundant climatic signals.

Interpretation: These results highlight that predictor independence is strongest in Punta Maroma, acceptable in Florida for hurricane effects, and more limited in Belize and the regional dataset due to redundancy in age. The site‑specific variability in concurvity emphasizes that model validity depends on local data structure, with Florida and Punta Maroma offering the most stable predictor independence, while Belize and Regional require more cautious interpretation.

While the temporal spline (s(age)) is not a predictor but rather a structural term that preserves the chronology of the record, its concurvity values indicate the degree of overlap between temporal trends and climatic/disturbance predictors. At Punta Maroma, negligible concurvity confirms that the temporal trend is independent of other covariates. In Florida and the Regional panel, higher concurvity for s(age) reflects overlap between temporal structure and climatic signals, while hurricane predictors retain independence. In Belize, moderate concurvity suggests partial redundancy between temporal and disturbance terms. Overall, these results show that temporal structure can overlap with climatic variability, but disturbance predictors remain sufficiently independent to justify their inclusion.
