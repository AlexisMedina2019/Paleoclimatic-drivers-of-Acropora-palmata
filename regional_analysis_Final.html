Abstract

This document is the executable record (Quarto/R) of all analyses reported in the main text. It uses three input files deposited in the repository: ages_kernel_reg_updt2.xlsx (dated Acropora palmata fossils, audited against the original age publications), df_GAM130326_regimes_v5.xlsx (centennial climate predictors) and Marine20_0-15ka.csv (the Marine20 radiocarbon calibration curve, used to propagate age uncertainty); Florida-proximal temperature records used for comparison only are in florida_local_SST_records/. From these it reproduces:

  1. binning of fossil ages into 0.1-ka (centennial) intervals, their alignment with climate predictors and the normalization of the response variable;
  2. kernel-density reconstructions of A. palmata age distributions for each reef site and for the regional composite, weighted so that they have the shape of the modelled series;
  3. exploratory analyses of predictor correlation and of the frequency of dated colonies across climate-regime combinations;
  4. beta-regression generalized additive models (GAMs) fitted separately to four reef sites (Punta Maroma, Florida, Belize, Saint Croix) and to a regional composite, first with one climate predictor at a time (marginal models) and then as a nested sequence of multi-predictor models, ranked by AIC and Akaike weights;
  5. diagnostics of the selected models (residual plots, concurvity, residual temporal autocorrelation) and sensitivity analyses;
  6. validation and robustness tests of the selected models (cross-validation against an s(age)-only reference model, circular-shift test, drop-one influence).

Code, outputs and a factual description of each result are shown together, so that every value reported in the main text can be traced to the code that produced it. Ecological interpretation is given in the main text.

REGIONAL ACROPORA PALMATA ANALYSIS

We import the fossil chronology (ages_kernel_reg_updt2.xlsx, sheet Registros) and retain A. palmata records (group == "palmata") dated 0–6 ka. The site colours defined here (custom_colors) are used in all figures.

Age reference, calibration and data corrections. The chronology was audited record by record against the original publications of each age (workbook coral_ages_audit_M3.xlsx; sheet Trazabilidad of ages_kernel_reg_updt2.xlsx lists, for every record, its source, method, published age, analytical uncertainty, calibration and the change with respect to the previous version, ages_kernel_reg_updt.xlsx). All ages are expressed in ka BP (before 1950 CE), the reference of the climate series, and all radiocarbon ages are calibrated with Marine20 using a local reservoir correction (ΔR), propagated in quadrature.

  • Punta Maroma. U-Th ages (Medina-Valmaseda et al. 2025) are published relative to the year of chemical separation (2021) and are converted to BP (0.071 ka younger). Ages from Blanchon et al. (2017) were already in years BP. One sample (SURF7(a), ~1956 CE) post-dates 1950; it keeps its age (−0.006 ka) and is assigned to the youngest bin. Two samples dated on separate subsamples (M1(a), M5(b)) retain both ages.

  • Saint Croix. Radiocarbon ages (Hubbard et al. 2005, 2013), originally calibrated with Marine98 and no local correction, are recalibrated with Marine20 and ΔR = 64 ± 32 ¹⁴C yr (DiNapoli et al. 2020). For samples without a published conventional age (Hubbard et al. 2013), the conventional age and its uncertainty are recovered as the values that reproduce the published calibrated age and 2σ range under the original calibration. One sample dated at two laboratories (BI2-05) enters once, with the error-weighted mean of its conventional ages; one published calibrated age inconsistent with its conventional age (BI1-13) is replaced by the recalibrated value; samples listed in both source publications are counted once. Four U-Th ages are not modified.

  • Belize. U-Th ages (Gischler et al. 2023) are in years BP and are not modified. Radiocarbon ages are recalibrated with Marine20 and ΔR = −248 ± 35 ¹⁴C yr (Greer et al. 2020), from the conventional ages in Gischler & Hudson (2004) or, for the atoll cores, from conventional ages recovered by inverting the original Marine98 calibration. Because this ΔR is a single, strongly negative estimate, we repeated the analysis using a regional ΔR of −114 ± 70 ¹⁴C yr (inverse-variance weighted mean of the ten nearest entries of the Marine Reservoir Database, with their standard deviation as uncertainty). Seven A. cervicornis samples previously coded as A. palmata were reclassified and excluded from the A. palmata series, and one typographical error in the compiled table was corrected from the original publication.

  • Florida. Radiocarbon ages were recalibrated with Marine20 by L. T. Toth (pers. comm.), and three samples absent from that compilation were recalibrated with the same local correction. U-Th ages were already in years BP. Seventeen rows that repeated a sample already present under another source database were removed. Records were standardized by collection (main reef tract, nearshore ridge, Dry Tortugas) as before.

  • Effect. A. palmata records in 0–6 ka: 257 → 236 (Punta Maroma 64, Florida 111, Belize 39, Saint Croix 22). Radiocarbon ages carry 95 % calibrated ranges of 0.3–0.5 kyr (3–5 centennial bins), so for the radiocarbon-dated sites the 0.1-kyr bin is an aggregation unit, not the resolving power of the records.

  • Calibration curve and age uncertainty. The age uncertainty shown in Fig. 2 is computed within this document, not taken from the source publications: the Marine20 curve (Heaton et al. 2020; https://doi.org/10.1017/RDC.2020.68), deposited in the repository as Marine20_0-15ka.csv (0–15,000 cal BP, columns cal BP, ¹⁴C age and its 1σ error), is combined with the conventional age, its error and the local ΔR with its error of each radiocarbon age (sheet Trazabilidad) to obtain its calibrated probability distribution, from which the ages are drawn (Section 1.4). No external calibration software is needed to reproduce it.

1.2 Climatic predictor: import & alignment

We import the centennial climate table (df_GAM130326_regimes_v5.xlsx) and assign each row the upper bound of its 0.1-ka bin. Ages are in ka BP (before 1950 CE), the convention of the two continuous climate sources (Kaufman et al. 2020; Schmitt et al. 2025).

Variables of the climate table. GMHT_CPS0_30N: temperature anomaly (°C, relative to 1800–1900 CE) of the Composite Plus Scale (CPS) reconstruction for the 0°–30°N latitude band of Kaufman et al. (2020). The band is a zonal composite of proxy records at all longitudes between the equator and 30°N, not a reconstruction restricted to the Atlantic–Caribbean. Of the 54 records of the Temperature 12k database in this band, 14 are from the Caribbean Sea, the Gulf of Mexico and South Florida; the others are from the eastern Atlantic and West Africa (5), the Indian Ocean, East Africa and the Arabian Sea (16), the western Pacific and East Asia (15) and the eastern Pacific (4) (files in ScientificDataAnalysis/lipdFilesWithEnsembles of the same repository). The anomaly therefore describes the tropical Northern Hemisphere as a whole, and its timing need not match that of the Caribbean. We use the median of its 500-member ensemble (file 0to30500-6bands-100yr2kwindow.csv of the authors’ code repository, deposited in kaufman2020_CPS/), whose 100-year bins are centred on 0, 100, … yr BP; the median is re-referenced to zero at 100 yr BP and linearly interpolated at the centres of our bins (50, 150, … yr BP). dT_raw: the centennial rate of change of that anomaly (δT), defined as the change from the preceding (older) century into each bin, δTj = Tj − Tj+1 (°C per century; positive values denote warming forward in time). For the oldest bin (5.9–6.0 ka), Tj+1 is the median at 6.05 ka (column GMHT_CPS0_30N_older). Round-3 correction. Table v4 used a single ensemble member (V1, shifted by a constant) assigned to our bins without interpolation (a half-bin offset), and computed dT_raw as Tj − Tj−1 (bin minus its younger neighbour), so that positive values denoted cooling, the value described the change that followed each century, and the youngest bin was set to 0. Both columns of v4 are kept in v5 (GMHT_CPS0_30N_V1_v4, dT_raw_v4); sheet CPS_traceability documents the rebuild, and Section 4.7.5 tests how the results depend on the choice of ensemble member. Hur100_fqcy: centennial tropical-cyclone (TC) frequency, the number of TC event layers in each 0.1-ka bin of the Great Blue Hole record (Schmitt et al. 2025). Hur100_av: smoothed centennial TC frequency, the mean of the two 100-year counts of the same record that overlap the bin (a centred 200-year smoothing). Both metrics count tropical storms and hurricanes; neither measures intensity. Schmitt et al. (2025, table S9) count events in windows of varve years before 2022 CE, so both metrics were recounted in our BP bins from their 50- and 100-year windows (a BP bin j spans 72 + 100j to 72 + 100(j + 1) years before 2022). As with the coral ages (Section 1.1), the youngest bin closes at the youngest dated colony (1956 CE): Hur100_fqcy counts 1850–1956 CE (15 events, one of them in 1956), and Hur100_av averages the windows 1800–1900 and 1900–2000 CE. Events after 2000 CE, including the 21st-century extrapolation of 45 events per century, are not used. The rebuild is documented bin by bin in sheet Hur_BP_traceability of the climate table; the earlier table (df_GAM130326_regimes_v3.xlsx) took the windows counted from 2022 CE in order, as if they were BP bins, which placed the counts about 150 years from the BP bins, and it had 45 events in the youngest bin. Counts recomputed from the individual event layers (Schmitt et al. 2025, table S4a) agree with the rebuilt series (r = 0.99; at most 2 events per bin apart). A sensitivity version that extends the youngest bin to 2022 CE as a rate per century (df_GAM130326_regimes_v5c.xlsx) is described in Section 4.7.1. Thermal, Hydro and Hurricane: categorical regimes (below). Hydro_FL, Hur_FL, FL_pact and FL_nrec: Florida series (below).

The three categorical regimes were coded from the published chronologies compiled in Supplementary Material 2, converted to ka BP, assigning each bin to the period that contains its midpoint (a midpoint on a boundary goes to the younger period):

  • Thermal (regional chronologies of the Caribbean and the Yucatán Peninsula compiled in Supplementary Material 2, independent of the 0°–30°N anomaly): warmer 0–0.1 ka (post-1850 CE); LIA 0.1–0.7 ka (~1300–1850 CE); CWP&MWA 0.7–1.3 ka (700–1250 CE); cooler 1.3–4.8 ka; warmer 4.8–6.0 ka (mid-Holocene warmth).
  • Hurricane (Schmitt et al. 2025 only, the source of Hur100_av and Hur100_fqcy): high 0–0.2 ka (rise after 1800 CE); calm 0.2–0.6 ka (1400–1800 CE); enhanced 0.6–1.1 and 1.2–1.4 ka (900–1400 and 600–800 CE); moderate 1.1–1.2 and 1.4–4.0 ka (mean 9–14 events per century); low 4.0–6.0 ka (~7 events per century; the record begins at 5.7 ka).
  • Hydro, by region. Yucatán (Hydro): wet 0–0.8 ka, dry 0.8–1.7 ka and wet 1.7–3.5 ka (Curtis et al. 1996, boundaries calibrated with IntCal20), wet 5.2–5.7 ka (Serrato Marks et al. 2021), no data elsewhere. Florida (Hydro_FL): dry 4.6–6.0 ka (Pollock et al. 2017; Donders 2014), wet 2.8–4.6 ka and dry 0–2.8 ka (Glaser et al. 2013), with multi-century droughts near 1.0 and 0.4 ka (Willard & Bernhardt 2011).
  • Hurricane, Florida (Hur_FL, FL_pact, FL_nrec). Schmitt et al. (2025) record storms reaching the Yucatán coast, and Florida-proximal records do not follow it (agreement in 46 % of the bins younger than 3.5 ka, no better than chance). The Florida panel therefore uses a local series built from five records on the Atlantic side of Florida: the western Great Bahama Bank / Florida Straits (Toomey et al. 2013; 0–6 ka), the Everglades (Yao et al. 2020; 0–3.5 ka), southwest Florida (Ercolani et al. 2015, via Oliva et al. 2017), and Blackwood Sinkhole (van Hengstum et al. 2016, via Oliva et al. 2017) and Thatchpoint Blue Hole (Winkler et al. 2020) on Great Abaco. An earlier record from Thatchpoint Blue Hole (van Hengstum et al. 2014) is not used, because Winkler et al. (2020) showed that its core spans only the last century; the bin-by-bin construction is in sheet Hur_FL_traceability of the climate table. All ages are cal BP (1950). For each bin, FL_pact is the proportion of the records covering the bin that report an active interval at its midpoint, FL_nrec the number of those records, and Hur_FL is “active” when FL_pact ≥ 0.5 and “quiet” otherwise. Older than 3.5 ka the series rests on a single record (Toomey et al. 2013). Records from the Gulf coast of Florida were not used, because they sample Gulf landfalls. For Hydro and the hurricane regime, the Florida panel uses the Florida series; Punta Maroma, Belize, Saint Croix and the regional composite use the Yucatán series (Hurricane = Schmitt et al. 2025). The continuous hurricane metrics (Hur100_av, Hur100_fqcy) are those of Schmitt et al. (2025) in every panel. In the Yucatán Hydro series, undocumented bins are coded as a separate level, “no data”, so that no bins are lost. The mapping is positional: interval_max is taken from the order in which interval labels first appear, which is correct only if the file has exactly one row per bin, ordered from youngest to oldest.

Two sources of thermal information. The thermal regime and the continuous temperature predictors come from different kinds of record, and they do not describe the same history. The regime follows regional chronologies, in which the Caribbean Warm Period–Medieval Warm Anomaly (0.7–1.3 ka) is warm and the Little Ice Age (0.1–0.7 ka) is cool. The CPS median for 0°–30°N shows no Medieval warm interval: its anomaly stays between −0.50 and −0.68 °C from 650 to 1250 CE. Its minimum falls at 0.4–0.5 ka (about 1450–1550 CE), and the anomaly rises from then on, so δT is positive in the three late Little Ice Age bins (0.1–0.4 ka) while the anomaly is still below zero. An early minimum is plausible for a band that includes much of the tropical Pacific, because the coldest phase of the Little Ice Age was not synchronous: it fell in the fifteenth century in the central and eastern Pacific, in the seventeenth century in northwestern Europe and southeastern North America, and in the mid-nineteenth century in most other regions (Neukom et al. 2019). The spread of the ensemble is also large relative to this signal (5–95 % range −1.9 to +0.3 °C at 0.45 ka), so the timing of the minimum is poorly constrained. The regime and the continuous predictors are therefore not interchangeable descriptions of temperature, and results based on one are not expected to reproduce those based on the other.

Climate values are joined to every fossil record by interval_max, and the column is renamed age (ka). The resulting merged_df is record-level and therefore holds climate values only for bins that contain at least one fossil record.

1.3 Response variable: centennial counts and normalization

The response variable of all models is built here, before any figure, so that Figs. 2 and 3 show the same series that enter the GAMs. This chunk makes three lasting changes to merged_df:

  1. Reference levels. Thermal, Hydro and Hurricane are converted to factors with reference levels “warmer”, “wet” and “low” (“quiet” for the local Florida hurricane series). These levels carry through the aggregation below and define every categorical contrast reported in Sections 3.2 and 3.3.

  2. Sites only. Rows are restricted to the four reef sites, which drops the duplicated “Regional” records created in the binning step. The regional composite is rebuilt from the site series below.

  3. Record counter. A column counts = 1 is added (one per dated record); it is summed per bin below.

The per-site objects (`datos_pm`, `datos_fl`, `datos_bz`, `datos_sc`, `datos_reg`) and `merged_df_g` created in this chunk are intermediate: all of them are redefined below, before any model is fitted.

Normalization

For each site, records are summed per bin (counts). Climate predictors are averaged within the bin, which leaves them unchanged because they are constant within a bin; regime labels are taken from the first record in the bin.

normalize_counts() divides counts by the site maximum (counts_norm, rounded to four decimals). Values exactly 0 or 1 are then transformed as y′ = (y(n − 1) + 0.5)/n, where n is the number of bins in the series (counts_norm_sv); all other values are unchanged. Only bins containing at least one record exist in the series, so no zeros occur and the correction affects only the maximum bin (e.g. 0.984 in Punta Maroma, n = 31).

The response is therefore the relative dated-occurrence frequency (hereafter frequency) within bins in which a site is represented: the number of dated colonies per bin, relative to the site maximum. It measures how often colonies of each century were found and dated, not population abundance, which was not measured. Bins without records are not part of any model: 29 of 60 in Punta Maroma, 36 in Belize, 41 in Saint Croix, 16 in Florida and 3 in the regional composite.

Florida source standardization

Florida combines records from several source datasets (column source). Counts are therefore first summed per source and bin, divided by the maximum of each source and boundary-corrected; the standardized values are then summed across sources per bin and normalized as above (datos_fl_std). Each source thus has equal weight at its maximum, independent of its number of records.

The regional composite used in every model is the per-bin sum of the four site-level counts_norm_sv series (Florida in its source-standardized form), normalized with the same function (datos_reg, n = 57 bins). Each site therefore enters the composite with its maximum bin equal to one, so its contribution depends on how its records are distributed among bins rather than on its number of records (Section 1.4). The five series are stacked into merged_df_g, which is used by every model and by Figs. 2 and 3a.

1.4 Acropora palmata age densities (weighted KDE)

Kernel density estimates (KDE) are computed from the individual ages, weighted so that the density has the shape of the modelled series of Section 1.3. Each record receives the weight it carries in counts_norm_sv: in Punta Maroma, Belize and Saint Croix, counts_norm_sv / counts of its bin; in Florida, the product of its source-standardization factor and the Florida normalization factor; in the regional composite, the site weight multiplied by the regional normalization factor. The weights of the records in each bin therefore add up to counts_norm_sv of that bin (checked in the code). For a site with a single source the weights are constant except in the maximum bin, so the weighted KDE has the same shape as the KDE of the raw ages; the weighting changes the shape only for Florida (source standardization) and for the regional composite.

Each density is a Gaussian KDE over 0–6 ka (bandwidth 0.09 ka, 512 points) with a reflection boundary correction (Silverman 1986; Jones 1993): ages are mirrored at 0 and 6 ka, so that the part of each kernel that would fall outside the window is folded back into it. Without the correction the density is underestimated within about two bandwidths of each edge, by up to one half at the edge itself, which would understate the modern peak and the density of the oldest centuries; with it each curve integrates to one over 0–6 ka. Post-1950 ages count at 0 ka, as in the binning. The bandwidth is a smoothing choice, not the resolving power of the record: the 95 % range of a radiocarbon age spans 0.3–0.5 ka (3–5 centennial bins), that of a U-Th age 0.02–0.05 ka. Two sources of uncertainty are estimated separately:

  • Sampling (which fossils were recovered and dated): 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the records with replacement, keeping their weights and stratified by source in Florida and by site in the regional composite. For each resample we keep the density curve and its mean in each 0.1-ka bin. signal_all holds the estimate and the 2.5–97.5 % and 25–75 % bootstrap envelopes of each curve; centennial_density_all holds the bin means of the estimate and their bootstrap SD and 95 % interval.
  • Age (where each dated fossil falls in time): 1,000 Monte Carlo draws of every age, with the record weights held at their central values; draws older than 6 ka leave the window. U-Th ages are drawn from a normal distribution with SD equal to half the reported 2σ error. Radiocarbon ages are drawn from their calibrated probability distribution, computed here from the conventional age, its error and the local ΔR with its error (combined in quadrature) under Marine20 (Heaton et al. 2020; https://doi.org/10.1017/RDC.2020.68; curve deposited as Marine20_0-15ka.csv), and shifted so that its median equals the audited age (the shift is ≤ 1 yr except for a few Florida ages calibrated with other software by their authors). The inputs are read from the sheet Trazabilidad of ages_kernel_reg_updt2.xlsx. Three Florida radiocarbon ages without a ΔR error take the median ΔR error of Florida. The 2.5–97.5 % range of the draws is the age envelope (age_q025, age_q975 in signal_all) and the 95 % range of each age is shown in Fig. 2.

Because a KDE integrates to one, the regional density distributes a fixed total among the four sites in proportion to the sum of their weights, which depends on how their records are spread among bins, not on how many records they have. Saint Croix, whose 22 records occupy 19 bins with at most two records each, carries 26 % of the regional weight with 9 % of the records, and Punta Maroma 12 % with 27 % of the records (site_share). This is a property of the per-site normalization used in the models, not of the KDE, and it applies equally to the regional GAMs.

3.2. Marginal GAM effects analysis

The relative frequency is continuous and bounded in (0, 1), so we fit GAMs with a beta distribution and logit link (mgcv::gam, family betar). Smoothing parameters are estimated by REML. Continuous predictors enter as thin-plate regression splines (mgcv default, basis dimension k = 10).

Climate regimes enter as factors with reference levels “warmer” (Thermal), “wet” (Hydro) and “low” (Hurricane). Their coefficients are contrasts on the logit scale relative to the reference, conditional on the other terms. Every model includes a smooth of age, s(age), which absorbs temporal structure not attributed to the climate predictor.

Modelling proceeds in two stages: marginal models, with s(age) plus one climate predictor (this section), and combined models, a nested sequence with two branches, one per hurricane metric (Section 3.3).

Candidates are ranked by AIC, ΔAIC and Akaike weights; models within ΔAIC ≤ 2 of the best are considered competitive. Marginal models are fitted without select = TRUE and with k = 10, whereas combined models use select = TRUE and a reduced k; AIC values are therefore not comparable between the two stages.

In this document, “linear” models are those whose climate term is a categorical factor (a parametric term), and “smooth” models those whose climate term is a continuous predictor fitted as a spline.

3.2.1 Candidate models

formulas_mg defines seven marginal candidates, each consisting of s(age) plus one of: s(GMHT_CPS0_30N) (M_temp), s(dT_raw) (M_dt), s(Hur100_fqcy) (M_hf), s(Hur100_av) (M_hav), Thermal (M_the), Hydro (M_hyd) or Hurricane (M_hur). fit_gams_panel() fits all candidates to one panel, and compare_gams() returns AIC, ΔAIC and Akaike weights.

Hydroclimate in panels with gaps in its series. The Yucatán hydroclimate series, used for Punta Maroma, Belize, Saint Croix and the regional composite, has no data for 20 of the 60 bins (level “no data”). That level is not a climatic state: as a factor level it contrasts periods with and without a hydroclimate record, and in preliminary fits it produced effects that had no climatic meaning (e.g. Saint Croix, where the hydroclimate model ranked first only through the “no data” contrast). In every panel whose series has such bins, the Hydro term is therefore removed from all candidates (drop_hydro()): M_hyd is not fitted, and in the combined sequence (Section 3.3) M5 is dropped and M6 and M7 keep the thermal and hurricane regimes without Hydro. Florida, whose hydroclimate series has no gaps, keeps all candidates. The marginal comparison therefore involves six candidates in Punta Maroma, Belize, Saint Croix and the regional composite, and seven in Florida.

3.3.2 Fitting procedure (ΔAIC and Akaike weights)

fit_combined_gams_safe() first removes Hydro where its series has gaps (drop_hydro(), Section 3.2.1) and then fits the remaining candidates to each panel in six steps:

  1. It removes rows with missing or infinite numeric values.

  2. It skips a model if n < 8 or n ≤ 3 × (number of smooths).

  3. It sets a common basis dimension for all smooths of a model, k = max(3, min(10, ⌊(n − 1)/number of smooths⌋)). For the four-smooth models this gives k = 7 in Punta Maroma (n = 31), 5 in Belize (24), 4 in Saint Croix (19), and 10 in Florida (44) and the regional composite (57). Nested models within a panel therefore do not share the same basis size (e.g. in Punta Maroma M1 uses k = 10 and M4 uses k = 7).

  4. It fits gam() (betar, REML) with select = TRUE, which penalizes each smooth’s null space so that a term can be shrunk to EDF ≈ 0. (bam() is used only when n > 100, which does not occur here.)

  5. If the beta fit fails, it refits the model as quasibinomial; such a model has no AIC and is excluded from the ranking.

  6. It excludes rank-deficient fits, i.e. models whose model-matrix rank is lower than the number of coefficients because two parametric terms are aliased. The names of the excluded models are returned in $excluded.

The function returns AIC, deviance explained, adjusted R², total EDF, ΔAIC and Akaike weights for each retained model; ΔAIC and weights are computed over the retained candidates only.

3.3.5 Saint Croix: limitations and decision

We do not draw site-level conclusions for Saint Croix. Its models are reported for completeness and labelled as not interpretable, for six reasons that concern the data rather than the results:

  1. Dating. All 22 ages are radiocarbon. The 95 % calibrated range of each spans 3–5 centennial bins (Section 1.4), so the bin of an individual record is uncertain: in 1,000 Monte Carlo draws of the ages, a Saint Croix age stays in its central bin in 37 % of draws on average (Punta Maroma, dated by U-Th: 87 %).
  2. Response. 16 of the 19 occupied bins hold a single record, so counts_norm_sv takes essentially two values (0.50 and 0.97; Section 3.1.3). The beta model then contrasts a handful of bins with two records against the rest.
  3. Marginal models. The best model (M_hav, weight 0.61) contains no significant climate term (s(Hur100_av), p = 0.076; Section 3.2.4), and neither does the best categorical model. Before Hydro was removed, the top-ranked model owed its rank only to the “no data” level of the hydroclimate series.
  4. Combined models. The selected model (M4_av) contains no significant climate term (s(Hur100_av), p = 0.054), none is significant when the response is modelled as one versus two records per bin (Section 4.5), and AICc selects M4_fqcy (Section 4.5.2).
  5. Age and sampling uncertainty. Across replicates of the ages and of the records, the best of the models without categorical regimes changes from replicate to replicate (the most frequent is chosen in 29–36 %), the climate models improve on s(age) in 43–53 % of the replicates and no climate term is significant in more than 28 % of them (Section 4.5.2).
  6. Density. At the narrowest bandwidth (0.05 ka) the age envelope of the Saint Croix density is as wide as its sampling envelope (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Saint Croix is kept in the regional composite, because removing a site after examining its results would be a post hoc selection and because it is the only eastern Caribbean record. Its weight in the composite (26 % of the weight with 9 % of the records; Section 1.4) is examined directly: the regional models are refitted without Saint Croix in Section 4.5.

4.5 Sensitivity analyses

We test the main results of the selected combined models against four alternative analyses:

  1. Residual autocorrelation (Florida, Punta Maroma). Florida, the panel with positive lag-1 autocorrelation (Section 4.4), and Punta Maroma, the panel with the strongest climate effect, are refitted as Gaussian GAMMs on the logit of the response, with the same terms and basis dimensions. Each is fitted first with independent errors and then with a continuous-time AR(1) structure (corCAR1 on age, because bins are not always consecutive). mgcv does not provide a beta family with correlated errors, so this is an approximation used as a check, not a replacement for the beta models.   2. Florida source standardization. The combined and marginal candidate sets are refitted to the Florida series pooled across sources (datos_fl_norm, Section 3.1.1).

4.6 Predictive validation and robustness of the coral–climate associations

Every model includes s(age), which represents the temporal structure of each series: the long-term trend and low-frequency variation in the frequency of dated colonies, including taphonomic and sampling effects that cannot be separated from ecological change. Climatic effects are therefore estimated conditional on s(age). All analyses in this section compare the selected models with a reference model, counts_norm_sv ~ s(age), and so ask whether climate adds information beyond the temporal structure of the coral record.

For each panel we evaluate three models: the reference model, the best marginal model (lowest AIC, Section 3.2) and the selected combined model (Section 3.3). Four analyses are run:

  1. Cross-validation. Each century is left out in turn, the model is refitted to the remaining centuries and the left-out century is predicted (leave-one-out, LOO). In a second version, the centuries within ±200 years of the left-out one are also removed from the fitting data (h-block), so that the prediction cannot borrow information from immediately adjacent centuries. Predictions are scored by their log-likelihood under the fitted beta distribution, and each model is compared with the reference model through the summed difference (ΔELPD; positive = better than the reference), its standard error and the percentage of centuries predicted better than by the reference.
  2. Circular-shift test. The complete 60-bin climatic series (all predictors together) is rotated in time by 6–54 bins (600–5,400 years; 49 shifts) relative to the coral record, while the response and s(age) stay fixed. Rotation keeps the shape and autocorrelation of both series and the correlations among predictors, and destroys only their alignment. The statistic is the AIC improvement over the reference model; p = (1 + number of shifts with an improvement ≥ the observed one) / (1 + number of shifts). The smallest attainable p is 0.02. Shifts of ≤ 500 years are excluded because neighbouring centuries are autocorrelated.
  3. Drop-one influence. Each century is removed in turn and the model refitted. For every climatic term we report its p-value with all data, the largest p-value across the drop-one fits, the century whose removal gives that largest value and the percentage of drop-one fits in which the term stays significant (p < 0.05).
  4. Former cross-validation (withdrawn). The five-fold cross-validation of the first revision is re-run to document why it was withdrawn (see below).

During refitting the basis dimension of each smooth is capped with safe_k_for() from the number of fitting observations, and further reduced if the total number of coefficients would reach that number. A refit is skipped, and the left-out century counted as not predicted, when the fitting data do not contain every level of a categorical predictor or the categorical design is rank-deficient. With the v5 climate table the full-data fits of the selected combined models are identical to those of Section 3.3 in every panel (same AIC). In Saint Croix (n = 19) the marginal smooths use k = 9 instead of the default 10, which changes the AIC of the marginal model by 0.01.

4.7.5 Choice of temperature reconstruction (CPS ensemble members)

4.7.6 Summary

Across the five tests, two results are stable. First, the frequency of dated colonies is highest in the two most recent centuries, which coincide with the “high” hurricane regime of Schmitt et al. (2025) and with the warm modern regime. This association holds across bin phases, time references, age and sampling uncertainty and temperature reconstructions, but it rests on two bins and cannot be separated from recency bias. Second, at Punta Maroma the LIA bins have a lower frequency than the modern century that follows them, but not a lower one than earlier cool intervals. In Punta Maroma the δT smooth selected with the CPS median and the thermal regime of the competitive models describe the same modern contrast; the regime is retained when both are present, and the smooth is selected with only 2 % of the ensemble members. The continuous temperature and TC predictors are not robust to bin phase, to age and sampling uncertainty or to the choice of reconstruction, and no climatic predictor shows a consistent association before 0.2 ka.
